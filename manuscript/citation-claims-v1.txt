Rapid growth in artificial-intelligence computing is increasing data-centre electricity demand and creating new generation, network-connection and local grid-operability challenges.
Temporal shifting of computing workloads can change electricity use and associated emissions, but its grid value depends on when and where the work is executed.
Credible data-centre flexibility depends on workload release times, computational requirements, service classes and deadlines rather than only on an assumed fixed flexible fraction of peak load.
Deferring computational work creates a future service and energy obligation, so successful power delivery during one event does not by itself establish recovery or repeatability.
Advance notice can increase demand-response capacity only when eligible work can be executed before the event using available compute and power headroom.
Flexible electricity demand can reduce renewable curtailment and expand the feasible integration of variable photovoltaic generation.
Flexible demand and battery energy storage can be complementary or substitutive because both act on a shared network- and energy-constrained feasible region.
Photovoltaic hosting capacity is jointly constrained by local demand, network limits, curtailment requirements and the timing flexibility of connected resources.
Coordinating heterogeneous demand-side resources across community units can mitigate city-scale generation-demand mismatches and influence grid-upgrade pathways.
Operational flexibility selected under one workload and community distribution requires independent local validation before transfer to a shifted operating distribution.
